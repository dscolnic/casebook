# The story each game tells

Fifteen days, three or four calls a day, one arc. These are not blurbs for a
store page — they are what actually happens across the fifteen day cards and
the stops under them, written down so that a day can be checked against the
story it belongs to. If a day stops serving the arc below, either the day is
wrong or this file is out of date.

Day cards live in `stake` on each mission. `GAMES.md` is the inventory;
`CLAUDE.md` is the working manual.

---

## The Contaminated City — *the fire is the easy part*

A freight yard burns beside a river and nobody can say what came off it. The
first week is the obvious emergency: name the chemicals from a burnt manifest
and three contradictory rumours, put a number on a plume drifting at two
neighbourhoods, and work out where the runoff went — which turns out not to be
the water, because the water tests clean while fish die in the sediment
downstream. By the fifth day the city is drinking its reserves, hospitals are on
tankers, and the player's numbers are what decide whether a water system reopens.
Then the site itself starts producing its own emergencies: two drains meeting in
a closed tunnel with a crew waiting to enter, a storage bay still warming
fourteen hours after the fire is out.

Halfway through, the story turns on the response. The treatment chemical that is
supposed to fix this is livelier in a warm vessel than anyone expected, and
treatment is what gathers a spill spread thin across a yard into a single tank.
The neutralisation dose works and pulls metals out of the pipework. The pH
correction works and moves seventy per cent of the contaminant into reservoir
sediment, where it waits for an autumn turnover. The pilot plant removes 98% of
the target compound and makes something the test panel was never written for.
Every fix relocates the problem, and the last three days are about admitting
that in public: an afternoon-and-overnight pattern in the air data that says the
event is not over, one verification result sitting just under a limit on a
re-run, and a mayor who wants one word when the evidence supports about eleven
different ones.

## Outbreak: Riverton — *you only see where you look*

Seven patients, three hospitals, four days, and a reporter downstairs who
already has the number. The opening week is a telescope pointed inward: is seven
even a lot, what counts as a case, what kind of agent is this, which cell does
it get into and how, and where in a cell's own production line is it
interfering. By day five a hospital system needs a working test by sunrise, one
control tube that should have been empty has come back positive, and three
thousand people have already been told to isolate on that run. Then the view
turns to patients — two people with identical labs going in opposite
directions, and a week later a ward full of people who improved and then
collapsed, with two consultants wanting the immune response suppressed by
midnight.

The turn comes on day eight. Ward isolation has held for nine days and cases
keep appearing anyway — and a sewer sample kept by chance was positive four days
before the first patient walked in. The outbreak did not start where the curve
says it started, which means every hospital-shaped answer has been a week late
from the beginning. From there the story widens faster than the response can
follow: sequences pointing up the river corridor while a council order to clear
a heron colony sits signed, a variant climbing in the one district whose
sampling changed, a therapy failing in one patient in four with resistant
sequence in a river sample banked before the first dose. It ends in a briefing
room where the mayor is on air in an hour, the animal source is still unsettled,
and one thing said in public early — that this began with the hospital cluster —
has to be corrected out loud.

## Deep Watch — *the boat is the other adversary*

You have the watch, you have been aboard four days, and Chief Ferro wants to
walk you through the forward spaces before it starts. The first four days are
about learning to read: what a bulkhead fitting is for, what a trace on a sonar
display is and is not, how far a current has carried a plot that has not been
fixed in three and a half hours, and what the boat can afford to switch off to
get through a four-mile-wide passage quietly. The recurring lesson is that
agreement is not evidence — two displays agree beautifully because they are fed
by the same gyro, and the depth under the keel is the only opinion aboard that
did not come from the plot.

Days five to eight are the casualties, and they teach ranking rather than
procedure: water over the bilge with the pump keeping level, smoke in a
compartment where an indicator lamp claims a bus is open and a meter reads four
hundred and forty volts, nine people asleep in a space whose sensor disagrees
with a handheld meter, and then all of it at once with a broken wrist in the
passage and not enough people. After that the boat becomes its own problem: it
is louder than the sea it is listening to, it is going deeper than ordered, a
bearing is running hot while the cooling pump draws its rated current, and the
quiet everyone wants costs the atmosphere and the cooling together. The last
three days are the quiet ones with the most at stake — a rig-for-dive sheet
signed by somebody who was ashore, one refit item the yard will fund against
four departments who all remember last patrol, and finally the watch the whole
patrol has been for, with everything degraded a little and nobody left to
delegate to.

## Bring Them Home — *the room stops fixing and starts counting*

Something exploded four minutes ago, a quarter of a million miles away, and
three consoles hold numbers that contradict each other. The first days are pure
diagnosis under a clock: decide what the vehicle is actually doing as opposed to
what the instruments say, rebuild where it is from a range, two angles and the
travel time of a radio pulse, and correct a drift that is small today and fatal
at the atmosphere — before the tracking station carrying the whole solution sets
in forty minutes. Then the crew has to turn the spacecraft by hand with the
guidance platform off and a thruster quad dead, and the story changes character.
Nothing is being repaired any more. Everything is being rationed.

The middle third is a consumables campaign: batteries days earlier than any plan
allowed, a bus sagging while current looks normal, a proposal to parallel two
modules at unknown states of charge inside a sealed cabin, a cabin at four
degrees with condensation running down the walls, and carbon dioxide rising past
a scrubber whose fan draws its rated current while moving almost no air. Then a
twelve-decibel fade takes the voice loop and the navigation measurement in the
same minute, and a burn has to be aimed by eye through a window reticle. The
ending returns to where it started, deliberately: several ground stations
suddenly agree that the trajectory has moved, and they are all fed from the same
clock — the same common-mode failure that opened day one. The final review has
to say how strongly each claim is held before eleven minutes of entry, four of
them without communications, and the legacy the board funds afterwards is
redesigning the shared dependencies that fooled the room twice.

## Planetary Defense — *the uncertainty is the antagonist*

A survey pipeline flags a faint point that moved between three exposures, in a
field with forty thousand stars, two satellite trails and a column of hot
pixels. The first four nights are about not fooling yourself: prove there is an
object, notice that one observatory's positions curve away from the fit in a
pattern, discover that every orbit fitting the arc draws almost the same track
across the sky while some of them pass a million kilometres away and some do
not — and then explain to the public why the impact probability went *up* this
morning, which is exactly what a shrinking uncertainty does while Earth is still
inside it. Nothing about the object is known yet except that it exists.

The middle third makes it physical, and each answer costs another: brightness is
size and reflectivity multiplied together, so the diameter is a range; the
absorption feature that would fix the composition sits where our own atmosphere
absorbs; the light curve repeats on almost the same interval as one
observatory's nightly window. A four-hour radar window that will not return for
eleven years arrives while the composition is still open. Then the consequences
land: an energy figure spanning two orders of magnitude because diameter enters
as a cube, three groups modelling the same entry and getting a ground impact, an
airburst and something between, and the discovery that this object came from the
sunward direction the survey never covers. It ends with a miss by two Earth
radii, an evacuation order that was drafted and never needed, and a survey
funding line expiring in eleven months — the most reliable consequence of a near
miss handled well.

## Project Y — *measure it before you build it*

Five divisions on a mesa that is not on any map, and three different notations
for the same nucleus on three blackboards. The first four days are a laboratory
teaching itself to measure: what a nuclide symbol specifies, where nuclear
energy comes from and why every energy figure is really a pulse height with a
calibration behind it, how to handle a sample that decays while the chemistry
runs, and when a difference in counts is real rather than the ordinary scatter
of a random process. Two architectures are on the table and the site can staff
one properly, which makes all of this a resource argument as much as a physics
one.

Then reactor plutonium arrives, and it is not the material the gun design was
built around. A process nobody had to care about produces a steady trickle of
neutrons on its own schedule, and a background rate turns out to be a deadline
in disguise — the whole site reorganises around compressing material inward, and
half of it has to be told why. The rest of the campaign is the consequence:
matter that rearranges itself under pressure, "nearly simultaneous" turned into
a number with a tolerance and an instrument that can see it, everything believed
about a thing that cannot be tested built out of mockups and high-speed imaging,
and a month where more data arrives and confidence goes down because the
measurements were never independent. Trinity is treated as an experiment with
its questions written down in advance, the observation lands outside the
prediction band, and the last day closes the technical reviews while the other
question stays open — the scientists who were on that mesa argued about it in
public for the rest of their lives, and never about the physics.

## Hospital Heroes — *your first shift to your last*

Three children arrive at almost the same time. One is quiet and breathing hard;
one is crying loudly about her ankle. That is the whole game in its first
minute: the loudest patient is not always the one in the most trouble, and
clues come in three kinds — what you see, what the patient tells you, and what a
tool gives you as a number. The next eight shifts walk the body one system at a
time, always through a child with a problem. Noah wheezes after cleaning a dusty
room and the sound says which part of the airway narrowed. Jordan's heart is
racing after the stairs, which is his body doing its job. Owen's sandwich is
taken apart in stages. Mateo says he feels fine, and two clues say otherwise.

The second half is about defences and judgement rather than plumbing. A scrape
is a hole in a wall that keeps everything out. Two tired children in the same
class are not necessarily the same illness, and *not enough evidence yet* is a
real answer. A shot is the body practising before the real germ arrives. Then
the shifts start asking for all of it at once: after a playground fall, the
scrape you can see matters less than the confusion you cannot; Chloe's swelling
lips outrank Sofia's itchy eyes even though Sofia has been miserable for weeks.
The last shift is the first one again with everything learned — six patients,
one free room, and then a handoff to an evening team that was not there for any
of it, which has to start with who and what is wrong rather than with the
answer.
