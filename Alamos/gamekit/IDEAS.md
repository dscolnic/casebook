# What to build next

`FOURTH_GAME.md` was about making a new game cheap. **`../GAME_IDEAS.md` is
about which game to make next** — new subjects, with the course, the place and
the argument worked out. This file is about making the games we already have
*better* — as teaching, and as things anybody would keep playing. Nothing
here is committed to; it is a list with the reasoning attached, so that a future
session can pick one up without re-deriving why it mattered.

Effort is rough: **S** an afternoon, **M** a day or two, **L** a week or more.

## Learning

### Retrieval practice from lessons that already exist
Each game's curriculum holds ~150 lessons and only 45–49 are reachable; the
rest are `— Review N` variants of the same content, generated by the importers
and never wired to anything. Make them optional call-backs — mission 7 re-asks
something from mission 2 for time rather than money, and a stop the player got
wrong comes back sooner than one they got right.

Spaced retrieval is the best-evidenced lever in learning science, and here the
content is already written and already validated. What is missing is a schedule
and a place in the mission flow to put it. **M, all four games.**

Note: the review lessons still carry their *old* short intro text — the
rewrite pass only covered the reachable ones. Wiring them in means writing
those too.

### Confidence, before the answer
One tap before submitting: sure / fairly sure / guessing. The verdict then knows
something it cannot know today — "you were sure, and wrong" is a different
teaching moment from "you guessed, and right."

Calibration is itself a scientific skill, this is a few lines in
`questionUI.js`, and it produces the signal every other item in this section
needs. **S.**

### Track which wrong answer, not that it was wrong
`state.missionResults` stores a boolean. The books already carry a rebuttal per
distractor (`ch.rebuttals`), so the information exists at the moment of the
mistake and is thrown away. Store the chosen index, and the end-of-campaign
debrief can say *"you took the loudest reading four times out of five"* — a
description of the player's reasoning rather than their score. **M.**

### Make gathering evidence cost something
Every DIAGNOSIS panel hands over all its readings for free. In the real work
the hard part is choosing what to measure: take a second reading, wait for a
trend, run a blank, send somebody to look. Give each a clock cost and let the
player decide how much certainty to buy.

This is the largest design change on the list and the one that most changes
what the games are about. It needs a per-format way to declare "extra evidence
available at a price", which `figures.js` and the instrument panels are already
close to supporting. **L.**

### Explain-back
After the verdict, three one-sentence accounts of *why* the answer is the
answer; pick the one that matches your reasoning. Catches right-answer /
wrong-reason, which the current grading cannot see at all. Cheap because the
books already write `why` and the rebuttals. **S.**

### Disagreeing people
Two crew members give conflicting accounts of the same event and the player has
to decide who to believe and on what grounds. The rosters carry three-paragraph
bios and roles already; this is content plus a small UI. Fits Deep Watch and
Riverton best. **M.**

## Feedback and the verdict

### A reasoning trail the last mission can use
Keep every call, its figure and its verdict for the whole campaign. Deep Watch's
final stop already asks the player to report to the captain — let that report be
assembled from what they actually recorded, uncertainties included. Riverton's
mission 15 ("Build the public chemistry narrative") wants the same thing. **M.**

### Show the near-miss, not just the miss
Ballpark already settles onto a log scale against the true value. Extend it:
which tile choice moved the answer, and by how much. The figure is there; the
attribution is not. **S.**

### A session report worth handing to a teacher
One button: questions asked, missed, time spent, calibration, and the
distractor pattern. This is what makes the games usable in a classroom rather
than demoable in one, and it is mostly a render of data the game already holds
(plus the two items above). **M — highest value outside this repo.**

## Fun and presence

### Passengers
Give somebody a lift and their passage quiz runs while you drive. Reuses
`personQuiz.js` and the new `engine/world/driving.js` with almost no new
machinery. **S.**

### Give the vehicles a job
Right now driving is free speed, and the clock is the whole game. A motor pool
checkout, a fuel or dispatch limit, roads that are faster than open ground —
any of these turns driving into a decision. **M.**

### Ambient radio
Dispatch traffic in Riverton, boat announcements in Deep Watch, a tannoy at Los
Alamos: short lines tied to mission state and time of day. The largest
atmosphere gain per line of code available anywhere in the repo. **S–M.**

### Weather and shift
Rain during the plume mission, night during the flooding mission. The sun angle
and the sky IBL already move with the clock (`updateTimeOfDay`), so this is
mostly authoring which conditions a mission runs under. **M.**

### Deep Watch: the periscope
One instrument you look *through*, with visibility that depends on depth and
sea state. The boat's own world can carry it. **M.**

### A mastery meter that cannot be farmed
Fill it from correct-first-time rather than eventual-correct. Visible progress,
honest signal, and it makes the retry economy mean something. **S.**

## Audience fit and access

### Reading-level variants
Hospital Heroes now measures at Flesch–Kincaid ~2.7 median (see below); the
same content could carry a grade 5–6 register for older readers, and Riverton
could use a lighter one for high school. The intro paragraph is one field —
this is a per-theme variant, not a rewrite of the game. **M per variant.**

### Comfort settings
Field-of-view slider, head-bob off, hold-to-sprint toggle. Motion sensitivity is
a real barrier to first-person software in schools and none of these is hard.
**S.**

### Reading comfort
A dyslexia-friendly face and looser line spacing as a settings toggle in the
question panel. **S.**

## Tooling — this multiplies everything above

### Put the one-off content checks into `npm run check`
Two scripts were written by hand during the intro rewrite and then thrown away:

* **answer-echo** — score each lesson's intro against its own correct answer for
  word overlap. It found six paragraphs that were steering the player straight
  at the answer, in content that passed every existing check.
* **readability** — Flesch–Kincaid per theme against a declared ceiling. The
  hospital's intros measured 7.2 median for an audience of eight-year-olds;
  nothing in the repo could see that.

Both belong in `engine/dev/` beside `validateContent.mjs`, with the ceiling
declared in the theme manifest. **S, and the first thing I would do.**

### Screenshot regression
The most expensive lesson in this repo is that everything visual passed every
assertion — a gable roof inside out in a shipped game, a sign behind a canopy,
a walk cycle whose feet travelled twice as far as the body. This week it was a
"windscreen frame" that turned out to be a solid slab forty centimetres from
the driver's face, found only by sitting in the seat.

A handful of fixed-camera renders, diffed per commit, would have caught every
one of them. **M.**

### Finish Project Y's person stops
Hospital and Riverton carry three-paragraph teaching bios plus a `quiz` array
per person, and `personQuiz.js` prefers those. Project Y still falls through to
the generated sentence-lift question. Same shape, just unwritten. **M, content.**

### The world forks
Still the last structural fork: the hospital's
build their places by hand, while both declare a site as data that nothing
reads. `FOURTH_GAME.md` has the detail. **L.**
