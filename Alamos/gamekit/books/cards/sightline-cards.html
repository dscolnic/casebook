1.2 One in six is not an error rate Identification Procedure · Identification Suite · a person · BALLPARK

Scene40w

Marcus Ilori, the identification procedures adviser, has the 4 December 2019 array record on the table. Six photographs were laid out together. The witness was asked whether the man she saw was among them. It was standard procedure that year.

Guide67w

Put a number on what the array asks. If the man she saw is not among the six and she picks anyway, the chance she lands on the suspect rather than a filler is one over the number of photographs. Pick the count and work it out. One tile is the number of fillers, which is what the suspect is being compared against rather than the divisor.

Ask23w

If the man she saw was not among the six and she picked anyway, how often would the suspect be the one picked?

Behind the button138w

What simultaneous presentation can change. Seeing all six photographs together makes side-by-side comparison easy, but a witness can still reject the array. A may-not-be-present instruction matters because it makes rejection explicit rather than implying that one of the six must be the answer.

What one in six means. If the offender is absent, the witness nevertheless makes a choice, and all six photographs are equally plausible, the suspect receives one of six choices on average. That is a conditional equal-choice baseline, not a measured false-identification rate and not a floor that every simultaneous lineup must exceed.

What the fillers do. Fillers who do not fit the witness's prior description make the suspect stand out. That can make the effective size much smaller than six, which is why the later mock-witness test matters more than the printed array size.

Takes as read

a lineup contains one suspect and a number of people known not to be the offender

Verdict131w

Six photographs shown together can encourage relative comparison: which face looks most like the memory. A witness can still reject the lineup, especially when explicitly told that the offender may be absent, which is why the missing caution matters. Put a number on it. If the man she saw is not among the six and she picks regardless, the chance she lands on the suspect rather than a filler is 1 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.17. That figure is only the equal-plausibility baseline. Fillers nobody would plausibly choose make the suspect stand out and can push the suspect-pick probability far above one in six. The protection therefore comes from fair fillers, a clear may-be-absent instruction, blind administration and a recorded immediate confidence statement — not from the printed number six by itself.

Takeaway22w

The printed size of a lineup is not a false-identification rate; fair fillers, instructions and administration determine how meaningful a choice is.

Ask40w

Behind the button198w

Why the two tasks come apart. Build, clothing, gait and hair are separate features, and any one of them survives a long look at low detail. Recognising a particular face depends on the relations between its parts — the spacing of the eyes against the width of the nose — and those relations blur first. A station scoring two out of twelve is therefore reporting no identification at all, and one scoring three is reporting almost none. Zero is not the benchmark, and treating it as one makes a witness sound better the further away they stood.

Why the distance is worth writing down. Testimony arrives as a description and an identification in one sentence, and a jury hears both as one claim. Knowing the distance at which naming failed in a controlled test on this same bay gives the court a number to put against the sentence rather than an impression.
