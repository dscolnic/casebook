1.1 Two terms and a fortnight Prediction Office · a room · DERIVE

Scene53w

Calloway, the chief tidal analyst, has the two biggest constituents on the board. One is a steady wave from the moon, one from the sun. This month both swing to the same height — the same amplitude. He wants the time from one spring tide to the next, using only these two waves.

Guide93w

You build the working one line at a time. At each step pick the expression that the line above actually gives you. Every wrong branch here is real math. It is not a typo, so do not hunt for one. Two constituents added together can be rewritten as a product. That is the product-to-sum identity — the rule that turns "wave plus wave" into "wave times wave." One factor is fast. One is slow. That slow factor is the envelope. It decides how often the two waves swing back into step: the beat.

Ask16w

Using the two constituents' speeds, how long is it from one spring tide to the next?

Behind the button183w

Why a product rather than a sum. Added together, two cosines of nearly equal speed give a wave. Its height rises and falls over days. Written as a product, that is explicit. One factor runs at the average speed. One runs at half the difference. The second factor is the envelope the first one runs inside.

Where the factor of two comes from at the end. The springs happen whenever the envelope is at its largest. The envelope is the size of the slow cosine rather than the cosine itself. A cosine reaches its largest size twice per period, once positive and once negative. So the interval between springs is half the period of the slow factor.

Why the amplitudes were made equal for this exercise. When the two amplitudes differ, the envelope never reaches zero. The arithmetic then acquires a term that carries no teaching. The real station has the moon term at 3.4 m and the sun term at 1.1 m. The interval between springs comes out the same. It depends on the two speeds and not on the two heights.

Takes as read

a cosine of angular speed ω has period 360° ÷ ω · two terms are in step when their arguments differ by a whole number of turns

Verdict80w

The interval comes out to 14.7 days. Two constituents of nearly equal speed, added together. That's the same as multiplying a fast wave by a slow one — the product-to-sum identity again. The slow factor's speed is half the difference of the two speeds: 0.51 degrees an hour here. So the slow wave's period is 706 hours. The springs arrive at every peak of that envelope's size, and that happens twice each period. Half of 706 hours is a fortnight.

Takeaway20w

A beat's interval comes from the difference between two speeds. Never from either speed alone, and never from the amplitudes.

2.1 Two rates make one speed Survey & Drift · Survey Pontoon · a room · DERIVE

Scene69w

Sunniva Palmer, the hydrographic surveyor, has fitted a position function for each coordinate — a formula that gives it at any time. That came from twelve hours of fixes. One gives the easting (how far east), the other the northing (how far north). Both are in metres, with time in hours from local low water, when the tide is lowest. She wants the speed at t = 2.0 h.

Guide73w

Palmer builds the working one line at a time. Each line has to follow from the expression right above it. Differentiate each coordinate on its own — that turns one path into two separate rates. Only after that do the two rates combine. They combine as the two shorter sides of a right-angled triangle, not by simple addition. Watch the inner factor on the easting closely: its angle is πt/6.2, not just t.

Ask11w

What speed was float 7 making at t = 2.0 hours?

Behind the button216w

Why each coordinate gets differentiated on its own. Position here is just two numbers that happen to share a clock — the easting and the northing. Nothing in the easting's expression shows up in the northing's. So each is differentiated by itself. The pair of results together is the velocity vector. That's the arrow made from both rates at once, pointing the way the float is actually moving.

Why speed throws away direction, but the vector itself doesn't. Velocity is a pair of numbers — the vector — and speed is just one. Squaring both parts, adding them, and taking the square root gives the length of that arrow. It carries no record of which way it points. That arrow does still point somewhere. It lies along the tangent, the exact direction the path is heading at that instant. Speed is what's wanted next, because the next step is accumulating distance along the path. A direction doesn't factor into a length.

What the inner factor is doing. The easting is a sine of πt/6.2, not of t. That's because the tide comes full circle every 12.4 hours. Differentiating it brings that π/6.2 out the front — a factor of 0.507 per hour. Forgetting it doubles the eastward rate to something this estuary has never actually recorded.

Takes as read

each coordinate can be differentiated on its own with respect to time · a rate of change, and the derivative as a rate — taken as read

Verdict78w

The speed is about 1,540 metres an hour. Differentiating the easting needs the chain rule, because it's a sine of πt/6.2, not of t. That inner factor is 0.507 per hour. It keeps the eastward rate at a believable 1,501 metres an hour. The northing is a polynomial. So its rate falls steadily, reaching 324 metres an hour at t = 2. Speed is the length of the vector those two rates make — squared, added, and rooted.

Takeaway23w

Differentiating a path means differentiating each of its coordinates on their own. Speed is the length of the pair those two rates make.

4.1 What twenty-nine terms can be worth Prediction Office · a room · DERIVE

Scene48w

Achterberg has the twenty-nine dropped constituents — the small waves left off the board. She has them listed by size. 0.041, 0.033, 0.028 and 0.022 metres, then twenty-five smaller ones adding to 0.066. Calloway wants to know the worst those twenty-nine could do to a predicted water level.

Guide79w

You build the working one line at a time. The remainder is the sum of the terms you left out. Each of those terms is an amplitude, a wave's height. Each is that amplitude times a cosine of some angle. You don't know what that angle is at the hour that matters. So the useful step is the one that stops needing to know it at all. A cosine can never be bigger than one, no matter its angle.

Ask14w

At any single hour, what is the most the twenty-nine dropped constituents could add?

Behind the button218w

Why a ceiling, not a guess. The twenty-nine dropped constituents each turn at their own speed. At any one hour, some of them add together and some cancel out. So the real sum is almost always much smaller than the worst case. But nobody knows in advance which hour will matter most. That is why the honest number to print is the ceiling. The largest it could possibly be. Not a guess at the likely value.

Where the triangle inequality comes in. The size of a sum is never bigger than the sum of the sizes of its parts. That is the whole trick used here. It is also why the answer doesn't depend on any of the twenty-nine timing angles. It works no matter what those angles turn out to be. That independence is exactly what makes the ceiling worth printing.

Why the comparison at the end is the real point of the day. A ceiling is only useful next to something else. Twenty centimeters either way is a lot of water in a shallow estuary. But it is almost nothing next to how far the level moves. That's true even over the forty minutes a stilling well takes to settle down. Knowing which of those two is bigger is what decides how the gates get programmed.

Takes as read

a cosine is never larger than one in size, whatever its argument · the size of a sum is at most the sum of the sizes of its terms · alternating series, and the error after n terms — taken as read

Verdict91w

The tail cannot go past 0.19 metres. Each dropped term is an amplitude times a cosine. A cosine never gets bigger than one in size. So each term is capped by its own amplitude, no matter its timing. Add those caps up and you get a ceiling on the whole tail. Not a guess, a hard limit. Most hours, the twenty-nine terms partly cancel and add only a few centimeters. What makes the ceiling worth having is that it holds at every hour. Nobody needs to know the timings at all.

Takeaway22w

A sum that stops early still has a ceiling on its error. That ceiling needs no knowledge of the dropped terms' timing.

The square in it is why the expansion has to be done before anything can be integrated.

Ask13w

What area does the ebb rose enclose, over the half turn it occupies?

Behind the button213w

Why a wedge and not a strip. A rose has no top and bottom, so a vertical strip cuts it in places that mean nothing. A wedge of angle dθ at reach r has area very nearly ½r²dθ. It is a triangle with base r·dθ and height r, and the error in that approximation vanishes faster than the wedge does.

Why the square is the awkward part. The reach is a sum of two terms, so its square has three: a constant, a cosine, and a cosine squared.
