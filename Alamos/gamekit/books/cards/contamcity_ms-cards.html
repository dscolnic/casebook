1.2 From damaged drum to a first answer Molecular Identification · Molecular Identification Lab · a person · SEQUENCE

Scene46w

Guide50w

Every one of these can be done right now. So this is not about time. It is about what you can still do afterwards. Ask what each step uses up. The markings? The air above the liquid? The drops? The sample itself? There is only enough liquid for two tests.

Ask13w

Put the four steps in order of what each one costs, cheapest first.

Behind the button153w

Why the order is graded whole. A sequence is a claim that each step needs the one before it to have happened. Get one pair the wrong way round and the claim is wrong, even if everything else lines up. That is why there is no part credit.

Why the ends are the place to start. The first and last steps are usually the easiest to be sure of, because one has nothing before it and the other has nothing after it. Fixing those two leaves fewer ways for the middle to go, and the middle is where the real question usually is.

What the rail can be ordered on. Often it is time. It can also be cost, or risk, or what you can still undo afterwards — do the steps that change nothing before the ones that change something for good. A step that destroys the sample can only be last.

Takes as read

some tests leave a sample as they found it, and some use it up

Verdict73w

The order is set by what each step costs. The markings on the outside survive only until somebody opens the drum, so they get photographed first, and it costs nothing. Sampling the air above the liquid takes what has already escaped, without opening anything. A test that gives the sample back can be repeated all week if it goes wrong. The test that uses the sample up answers best, and it answers once.

Takeaway14w

Do the tests that cost nothing before the ones that cannot be taken back.

Takes as read

some things dissolve in water and some react with it

Verdict153w

Water is the usual answer to a spill because it is what everybody has. For most of a yard it is the right one. A salt or a weak acid gets weaker as more water arrives, so diluting it helps. The label is the only thing that says which list a drum is on, which is why a yard of drums with no labels is the emergency and not just a nuisance.

4.2 Say what should happen, then check Quantitative Analysis · Quantitative Analysis & QA · a person · VERIFY

Guide56w

Lock the reading you expect after watering the sample down by half, before you do it. Then water it down and see what the meter says. Checking costs a verification slot, and spending it is the point. A meter prints a number for almost anything, and only the comparison shows whether this one can be trusted.

Ask14w

What should the reading become after watering it down, and does the rerun agree?

Behind the button132w

Why watering it down is the test. If the reading really comes from this chemical, then half as much of it should give half the reading. That holds while the reading is inside the range the meter was calibrated for. Outside that range it does not halve, because the relationship there has stopped being straight.

What a reading outside the range is worth. The meter still prints one, with just as many digits and no warning. It is a guess dressed as a measurement, and a check like this one is the only way to catch it.

Why you lock the prediction first. Reading the watered-down sample and then deciding what you expected is not a test. Doing it in that order is what makes the second reading evidence instead of agreement.
