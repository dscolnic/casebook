Mechanical

One edit each, no judgement call in any of them — a glossary line, a caption cut, a stale debt entry deleted. Cheapest work in the document and it closes whole gates.

delivery

1 finding
What it checks

The campaign builds one named thing, one piece a day, on a board in a room the player can walk into. The opening card names it, and every piece caption fits a board cell.

Why it exists

A campaign whose opening card never names what is being built tells the player the stakes and not the job. A caption too long for its cell is invisible on the board and renders perfectly in a screenshot.

What The Trial owes it
  • The opening card never names The Monitoring Board Pack: the player is told what is at stake and not what they are building.

checkStory

80 findings
What it checks

The story contract. The opening card’s four beats and its five-sentence cap; a day’s stake and briefing inside four sentences; a reason line under every call saying why it matters today; no cast names on the plan card or the debrief; a closing card with a turn in it; an ending addressed to the player.

Why it exists

A campaign can be correct and still read as a list of problems. This is the gate that says so, and it is the largest single debt in every book in the bundle.

What The Trial owes it
  • 48 calls carry no reason line.
  • 15 day cards name somebody on the plan card or the debrief.
  • 15 opening blurbs run past four sentences.
  • 2 cards read as reference rather than prose.

Read this firstThe largest story debt against the plainest prose in the repo. Nothing here is a reading-level problem; it is all card shape.

validateContent

2 findings
What it checks

The content invariants, plus a reading-level ceiling per card at the campaign’s own declared grade. Every lesson has a real scene, takeaway never repeats why, choices contains correctChoice verbatim, and no scene or verdict reads above audience.grade.

Why it exists

The invariants are what make a card gradeable at all. The grade ceiling is the softer half and the one that fails here: a card written for a graduate reader inside a course declared for grade 12.

What The Trial owes it
  • “What needs extra escalation today”: scene at grade 16.2 and verdict at 16.0, theme declares 12.

Read this firstOne card, both halves, and the two hardest-reading passages in a campaign whose mean is 4.7. Worth reading as an outlier rather than a trend.

probeQuestions

1 finding
What it checks

Probes on the shape of an answer rather than its content, reported in three classes. LEAK — the keyed answer gives itself away: either the takeaway, shown before the question, repeats its words, or its length alone identifies it. ORDER — a sequence’s prose fixes its own order, so the science is not what settles it. ECHO — the prompt’s own content words reappear in the keyed option.

Why it exists

Every one of these lets a reader answer correctly without understanding anything. The length signal was 88 per cent of one game’s passage quizzes before it was measured.

What The Trial owes it
  • LEAK, by length — M15.3 “Sign the board pack”: the keyed answer is 2.1× the length of a typical distractor, so length alone identifies it.

conceptVisible

8 findings
What it checks

Two questions about the same thing. Per card: is the stop’s own syllabus concept named in the question or one of the options — the only text a player has in front of them at the moment they answer? And per campaign: does every syllabus concept reach at least one question that way? Three findings: UNNAMED, a card that names its concept nowhere in the ask or the options; BURIED, the same card also carrying four or more of the campaign’s own glossary terms, which is the detail crowding out the subject; UNREACHED, a concept no question in the campaign says out loud.

Why it exists

A concept is assigned in metadata and taught in the scene, the guide and the background — and a card can do all of that and then ask a question in which neither the idea nor any of its words appears. The player answers correctly, every other gate is green, and what they practised was reading comprehension. This gate reads only the ask and the options, deliberately, because the other four zones are where a concept hides.

What The Trial owes it

  • UNNAMED — “Reading the same files twice”: asks about "Measurement error, rater drift and inter-rater agreement" and names it nowhere in the question or the options

  • UNNAMED — “A trial ends at a number of events”: asks about "Event-driven trials, and information fraction as progress" and names it nowhere in the question or the options

What the importer refused, and what was changed to get past it

None of these are content gates — the importer writes nothing at all while any of them stands, so the campaign could not ship until each was resolved. They are listed here because the same class has blocked every bundle so far, and an author regenerating this book from the same source will reintroduce them unless they are fixed upstream. The full catalogue, with the rule behind each, is in Bundle Pre-Flight.

  • None recorded for this campaign.

What changed under these numbers

  • The book and theme.js were replaced from the revised bundle and every content file regenerated by tools/import-book.mjs.
  • The opening card now has a five-sentence cap in checkStory, counted by the same splitter as the day blurb’s four-sentence cap — one function in engine/dev/dayCard.mjs, with a selftest that fails if the two ever disagree. The 48 campaigns whose openings predate the cap are recorded in engine/dev/openinglength-debt.json.
  • Where this campaign’s opening ran long it was cut to five sentences. That trade is visible in plainCards: fewer sentences carrying the same four beats means longer sentences, so the reading grade moved up slightly on some cards. The cap and the reading level pull against each other and both numbers are above.
